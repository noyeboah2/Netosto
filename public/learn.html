    What is sickle cell disease?

    [[sickle-cell-disease|Sickle cell disease]] is an inherited blood disorder. It is caused by a change in the [[hbb-gene|HBB gene]], which holds the instructions for part of [[haemoglobin]], the protein that carries oxygen in [[red-blood-cell|red blood cells]].

    The changed gene makes [[haemoglobin-s|haemoglobin S]]. After releasing oxygen, haemoglobin S can clump into stiff fibres ([[polymerisation]]). This bends red blood cells into a curved sickle shape. Sickled cells are rigid and sticky, can block small blood vessels, and die after 10 to 20 days instead of about 120.

    A person has sickle cell disease when they inherit an S gene from both parents (genotype SS). With one S gene (AS), a person has [[sickle-cell-trait|sickle cell trait]] and usually has no symptoms. Other forms exist too, such as SC disease, where S is inherited with another haemoglobin change.

    How this model works

    This is a simplified educational model, not a medical prediction. The share of sickled cells sets how many can become trapped in the narrow section. Blood flow is calculated as 1 − 0.85 × (trapped cells ÷ maximum trapped cells), so a fully jammed capillary still lets about 15% through.

    Real blood flow also depends on vessel size, blood thickness (viscosity), how sticky cells are to vessel walls, inflammation and hydration. Engineers and scientists build models like this, with many more variables, to study diseases and test treatments.

    Signs and symptoms

    Symptoms usually begin in early childhood, often around 5 to 6 months of age, and vary a lot from person to person.

    • Pain crises: sudden pain in bones, joints, chest or belly when sickled cells block blood flow ([[vaso-occlusion]]).
    • [[anaemia|Anaemia]]: tiredness, weakness, pale skin and shortness of breath.
    • Swelling of the hands and feet, often one of the first signs in babies.
    • Frequent infections, because the spleen is damaged.
    • Yellow eyes or skin (jaundice), from red blood cells breaking down quickly.
    • Delayed growth in some children and teenagers.

    Common triggers

    These can make sickling more likely. Avoiding them does not cure the disease, but it can reduce crises.

    Dehydration

    Not drinking enough water, especially in hot weather or during sport.

    Low oxygen

    Hard exercise without rest, high altitude, or breathing problems.

    Temperature extremes

    Getting very hot, very cold, or moving suddenly between the two, like jumping into cold water.

    Infection and stress

    Illnesses such as malaria or flu, and physical or emotional stress.

    Emergency warning signs

    For someone with sickle cell disease, these need medical care right away:

    • Fever (38.5°C / 101°F or higher)
    • Chest pain, coughing or trouble breathing
    • Severe headache, sudden weakness, trouble speaking or a drooping face (possible stroke)
    • Severe pain that does not get better with usual medicine
    • A suddenly swollen belly, or looking very pale and tired
    • A painful erection lasting more than a few hours

    Treatment and care

    There is a lot doctors can do, and care starts best when the disease is found early through [[newborn-screening|newborn screening]].

    • [[hydroxyurea|Hydroxyurea]]: a daily medicine that raises [[fetal-haemoglobin|fetal haemoglobin]] and reduces crises.
    • Folic acid: helps the body make new red blood cells.
    • Penicillin and vaccines: protect young children from serious infections.
    • Malaria prevention: sleeping under treated bed nets is especially important.
    • [[blood-transfusion|Blood transfusions]]: for severe anaemia, stroke prevention and some complications.
    • [[stem-cell-transplant|Stem cell transplant]]: can cure the disease, but needs a matched donor and has serious risks.
    • [[gene-therapy|Gene therapy]]: approved in the United States in 2023; very expensive and not yet widely available.

    Myths and facts

    Myth

    You can catch sickle cell disease from someone who has it.

    Fact

    It is inherited through genes. It is not contagious at all.

    Myth

    It is caused by a curse, a spirit or something the mother did.

    Fact

    It happens when a child inherits an S gene from both parents. Nobody is to blame.

    Myth

    People with sickle cell trait (AS) have the disease.

    Fact

    People with AS are carriers and usually healthy, but they can pass S to their children.

    Myth

    People with sickle cell can’t go to school, work or play sport.

    Fact

    With good care, hydration and rest when needed, they can live full, active lives.

    Myth

    Children with sickle cell always die young.

    Fact

    Early diagnosis, infection prevention and modern treatment help many people live into adulthood and old age.

    Know your genotype

    A simple blood test can show whether you are AA, AS or SS. Knowing your genotype, and your partner’s, helps couples understand the chances for their future children and make informed decisions together.

    Two people with AS have a 1 in 4 chance of having a child with sickle cell disease in each pregnancy. Ask a doctor, clinic or health worker about genotype testing.
